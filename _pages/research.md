# Thoughts on Research

Neuroscience contains many theories, each capturing part of the truth, yet few seem sufficiently general or easily falsifiable. I believe that truly proposing a new theory requires more than confidence in one’s own ideas — it also requires a deep understanding of existing theories, including where they succeed and where they fail.

Among the many layers of neuroscience, electrophysiology stands on particularly solid ground. At the levels of observation, modeling, and application, we already have remarkably successful frameworks. Neural pathways and circuit mechanisms are also largely reliable, although many details remain incomplete. However, when it comes to higher-level brain functions — fine motor control, emotion, or even consciousness — our understanding is still very limited.

My own research journey is only beginning. I do not expect to immediately discover a complete theory of the brain, although I believe such understanding is ultimately possible. Human beings have already explained many phenomena once considered impossibly complex. For now, I am more interested in approaching the problem from the perspective of prediction: as larger and richer datasets become available, perhaps we can observe new structures, constraints, and principles hidden within neural systems.

I often feel that modern research moves too quickly toward immediate solutions, applications, and translation. Much of it is problem-oriented. My own preference is more object-oriented: to find a meaningful object of study, continuously uncover its properties, and understand it more deeply. I believe that sufficiently deep understanding of an object will eventually lead to new theories. The person who ultimately formulates those theories may not be me — it may be another Newton in the future — and I would still consider that deeply worthwhile.

> Scientific research asks what is true.
> Art and advertising ask what looks convincing.
